The Location

Why this site is wrong

Two campuses. Nearly 1,700 students. A jail facility with a daily release population. A corridor with no transit, no walkable services, and no support infrastructure. Every claim below is sourced from official county documents, local news coverage, or statements from public officials.

The site is about a third of a mile from two schools — and a proposed third school

George Wagner Middle School (969 students) and Mitchell Elementary (719 students) are both approximately a third of a mile from the proposed site at 1200 CR 110. Together, nearly 1,700 students attend these two campuses — an 8 to 10 minute walk from the facility entrance. A proposed IDEA Public School campus, shown on county planning maps in the same corridor, is also about a third of a mile away.

County site plan. Orange: Phase 1 Justice Campus development area. Green: purchased property (253 acres, $75.8M). Blue: existing Wilco government facilities. Red boxes: Elementary & Middle School campuses and proposed IDEA Public School site. Source: Williamson County Justice Complex Strategic Planning →

This is a jail, not a prison — people are released daily

County jails hold individuals awaiting trial and process same-day bookings. Unlike state prisons, they have a daily release cadence. People leave at all hours, often with no transportation arranged. The operational reality of a county jail is fundamentally different from a state correctional facility.

There is no infrastructure for released inmates at this location

The CR 110 / SE Inner Loop corridor has no fixed public transit route, no walkable services or businesses, and no support resources. CARTS, the local demand-response transit service, requires advance reservations and a phone to schedule a ride. Someone released with no phone and no money cannot use it. They have nowhere to go but into surrounding neighborhoods and school zones.

City officials confirmed inmate release is already a documented downtown problem

A City of Georgetown official confirmed in writing that "what happens to individuals when they leave the jail has been a major issue in our downtown area" and that the city is actively working with the county on it. Moving the facility to a residential corridor next to schools does not solve the problem — it relocates it.

Process Concerns

The Public Was Locked Out

By the time residents found out, the deal was effectively done.

  1. March 9, 2026

    A purchase contract for 253 acres is signed — $75.8 million for land next to two schools. The named buyer is a private individual acting as the county's paid real estate consultant. No public announcement.

  2. March 10, 2026

    Commissioners go into closed executive session. One day after the contract was signed.

  3. March 24, 2026

    The public vote. The agenda item reads: "Assignment of Commercial Contract — Unimproved Property." No price. No mention of a jail. No mention of the schools nearby.

    A resident at the meeting had to ask out loud what the 248 acres was even being purchased for. The county's response: "We'll talk about it when we get to that item."

    The vote passed 5-0.

What residents weren't told before the vote

The price. The purpose. The location relative to George Wagner Middle School and Mitchell Elementary. The 1,700 students on those two campuses, a third of a mile from the property line. The affected neighborhoods — Carlson Place, Saddle Creek, Pinnacle, Churchill Farms, Summercrest, and University Park — found out only after the deal was already done.

What the agenda was supposed to do

Texas law requires meeting agendas to be specific enough to inform the public. A $75.8 million jail purchase next to two schools is exactly the kind of decision residents deserve to know about in advance. The agenda didn't come close.

The county's own press release the next day described the project in full — the price, the address, the parcel numbers, the Justice Complex purpose. That information was available. It just wasn't in the agenda.

The result

By the time anyone in the neighborhood knew what had been decided, the contract was 15 days into a 75-day option period. The county didn't hold a public information session on the project until April 21 — four weeks after the vote. The public had no real chance to weigh in before the decision was effectively made. That's not how this is supposed to work.

The good news: the option period is still open. The county can still walk away from this site. But not unless residents make it impossible to ignore.

Legal Notice · May 6, 2026

An attorney has put the county on notice

On May 6, 2026, Bill Aleshire, a prominent Texas open government attorney, sent a formal letter on behalf of Georgetown resident James Headrick to Williamson County Judge Steve Snell and County Attorney Dee Hobbs.

The letter says the March 24 vote to purchase 253 acres for a county jail violated the Texas Open Meetings Act. The agenda item didn't mention a jail. It didn't mention the price. It gave residents no reason to show up and speak.

Under Texas law, when a vote is of "special interest to the public," the agenda notice has to say so. A $75.8 million land purchase next to two schools, paid at more than eight times its appraised value, to house a 3,000-bed jail facility, qualifies.

The letter isn't a lawsuit. It's a formal request for the county to do the right thing: put the item back on the agenda with a description that actually tells people what they're voting on, then hold a proper vote.

That's not a radical ask. That's how this is supposed to work.

The county has a choice to make. If they repost the item correctly and let residents weigh in before the vote, we'll be there. If they don't, the legal options get more serious.

Watch next week's Commissioners Court agenda. If this item appears, show up.

The Price

$75.8 million for land WCAD values at $9.2 million

Williamson County paid 8.3 times what the appraisal district says these four parcels are worth. They paid more than ten times what a 481-acre rural alternative would have cost. They picked the most expensive option Kitchell put in front of them — and put it next to two schools.

The county is paying 8.3 times the appraised market value

Williamson County paid $75,819,874 for four parcels the Williamson Central Appraisal District values at $9,160,289 combined. That is a $66.66 million premium over appraised value — roughly 8.3 times the WCAD valuation.

The four parcels are R038820 (1200 CR 110, $4,908,531), R519352 (CR 110, $48,588), R432645 (3011 Maple St, $426,301), and R519356 (2180 Sam Houston Ave, $3,776,869).

All four parcels carry active agricultural exemptions, which can hold appraised values below open-market sale prices. WCAD market value is not identical to fair-market sale value. But an 8x gap warrants scrutiny — particularly when commissioners declined to even investigate a 481-acre rural alternative priced at approximately $7 million eight months earlier.

Source: Williamson Central Appraisal District (search.wcad.org) →

Kitchell identified 10 qualifying sites — the county paid $75.8M for this one

Kitchell, the county's project manager, evaluated 35-37 candidate sites and identified 10 that met the county's criteria. In July 2025, commissioners split 2-2 on entering a contract to begin due diligence on a Florence property near Highway 195 in the Georgetown ETJ — 481 acres priced at approximately $7 million, one of those 10 qualifying sites. The motion failed. Eight months later, commissioners unanimously approved a contract to purchase 253 acres on CR 110 for $75,819,874. That is a $68 million difference in land cost, for less acreage, placed next to two schools.

To be precise: the July vote was specifically against entering a contract to begin due diligence on the Florence site — not a vote against the location itself. Commissioners disagreed on whether a contract was needed before doing site evaluation.

Our Position

We support the county. We're asking for better answers.

Williamson County needs a modern jail. The current downtown facility is outdated. This is not a campaign against the project itself.

What we're asking is why commissioners unanimously approved a site next to two schools and residential neighborhoods, when there is so much open land available in the county. And what the county's plan is for daily inmate releases into neighborhoods with no transit, no services, and students walking by.

"County convenience shouldn't take precedence over resident safety. There is plenty of open land in Williamson County that meets every criteria without being next to schools."
Georgetown resident and concerned parent

Agendas post the Wednesday before each meeting on the county website and on the courthouse bulletin board. To speak, sign up using the public participation form.
